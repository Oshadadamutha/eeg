01 The Hypothesis

A robust AI model should generalize to new data it has never seen. In BCI, this means calibrating on one day and expecting it to work on another — the most critical real-world requirement for any practical Brain-Computer Interface.

This simulates the real scenario: a user calibrates a BCI device on Monday and expects it to work on Tuesday. If the model can't generalize across sessions, it has zero practical value.

03 The Failure

The results were stark. The model achieved near-perfect training performance but completely failed on the new session — performing no better than random chance.

Train Accuracy
>90%
Sessions 1 & 2 — memorized
Test Accuracy
~16%
Session 3 — random chance (6 classes = 16.6%)

With 6 classes, a random guess yields exactly 16.6%. The model was performing at pure chance on the new session — meaning it had learned zero transferable knowledge.

04 Root Cause Analysis: EEG Non-Stationarity

A deep dive into the BCI literature uncovered the root cause. EEG signals are non-stationary — their statistical properties change over time due to multiple physical and physiological factors.

01

Electrode Impedance Changes

The conductivity of the conductive gel changes as it dries between sessions, shifting signal amplitudes and channel characteristics.

02

Headset Cap Displacement

Slight shifts in electrode position between donning/doffing the headset change the spatial mapping of EEG channels to brain regions.

03

User Fatigue & Cognitive State

The brain produces measurably different signals when tired vs. alert. Session 3 (a different day) represents a completely different cognitive baseline.

The Hard Problem of BCI

Cross-Session Transfer

The model had learned the specific "noise fingerprint" of Sessions 1 & 2, but those fingerprints don't exist in Session 3. This is a known hard problem in BCI research. Solving it requires either domain adaptation (Phase 3) or randomized trial splitting to first establish a learnability baseline — which motivates Week 5's approach.
