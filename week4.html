Week 4: The "Session Transfer" Crisis

The Non-Stationarity Problem – Why Brain Signals Drift

The Hypothesis

A robust AI model should work on new data it has never seen before. In the context of this project, that meant:

  • Train on Data from Sessions 1 & 2.
  • Test on Data from Session 3 (recorded on a different day).

This simulates a real-world scenario where a user calibrates a BCI device on Monday and expects it to work on Tuesday.

The Experiment

I used my load_mi_data function to split the files strictly by filename:

# Strict Session Split
X_train, Y_train = load_mi_data(data_directory, session_patterns=['session1', 'session2'])
X_test, Y_test = load_mi_data(data_directory, session_patterns=['session3'])

The Failure

The results were shocking:

  • Train Accuracy: >90%
  • Test Accuracy: ~16%

Since there are 6 classes, a random guess would yield 100 / 6 = 16.66%. My model was performing no better than chance on the new session.

Root Cause Analysis: EEG Non-Stationarity

I dove into the literature to understand this failure. EEG signals are non-stationary, meaning their statistical properties change over time due to:

  1. Electrode Impedance: The conductivity of the gel changes as it dries.
  2. Cap Displacement: Slight shifts in the headset position change the "spatial" mapping.
  3. User Fatigue: The brain produces different signals when tired vs. alert.

The model had learned the specific "noise fingerprint" of Session 1 and 2, but those fingerprints didn't exist in Session 3. This is a known "Hard Problem" in BCI research known as Cross-Session Transfer.
